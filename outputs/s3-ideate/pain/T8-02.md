# T8-02 pain: proxy admin, money and estate half

## Pain items

### 1. Banks reject or stall a valid POA
- Who: POA agent (adult child)
- What hurts: Institutions insist on their own POA form, demand physician letters for "springing" powers, or simply refuse the document, even though state law generally requires acceptance absent a specific legal reason.
- Frequency: Widespread per legal-practice sources; no national rejection rate found; CFPB has fielded enough cases to publish two standing consumer-answer pages on the topic.
- Cost (time/money): Delays measured in months in reported cases (see item 2); often requires an elder-law attorney to intervene.
- Current workaround/incumbent: Presenting the POA to the bank before it's needed; hiring an attorney to write a demand letter; opening accounts at banks known to be POA-friendly.
- Evidence: "My family member signed a power of attorney (POA) but when I took it to the bank/credit union, I was told the POA has to be on the bank/credit union's form. What can I do?" (https://www.consumerfinance.gov/ask-cfpb/my-family-member-signed-a-power-of-attorney-poa-but-when-i-took-it-to-the-bankcredit-union-i-was-told-the-poa-has-to-be-on-the-bankcredit-unions-form-what-can-i-do-en-1151/), undated CFPB consumer-answer page.
- Severity: 4 — blocks legitimate access to funds needed for daily bills, sometimes for months.

### 2. Housebound or incapacitated parent denied funds despite valid POA
- Who: Adult child / POA agent, housebound parent
- What hurts: Some banks will not process transactions without the account owner physically present, even when the owner cannot travel, leaving bills unpaid.
- Frequency: unknown (isolated reported cases)
- Cost (time/money): One documented case ran seven months without pension access.
- Current workaround/incumbent: Escalation to bank ombudsman, media pressure, switching institutions.
- Evidence: "went without her pension money for seven months, because RBC refused to cash the cheques" (https://amp.cbc.ca/lite/story/1.1210222), reporting on a 94-year-old housebound client.
- Severity: 5 — direct loss of income access for a vulnerable person over an extended period.

### 3. Confusion between convenience/authorized signer, joint owner and POA
- Who: Adult child helping with banking; parent
- What hurts: Families default to being added as a "joint owner" when a limited convenience-signer role would fit, creating Medicaid asset-counting exposure and post-death ownership disputes, because bank staff commonly set up the wrong relationship type.
- Frequency: "In most cases, banks and other financial institutions add an individual to an account as a joint owner, not an authorized signer" — described as the common outcome, not the exception.
- Cost (time/money): Can disqualify a parent from Medicaid until a joint balance is spent down; unknown dollar figures.
- Current workaround/incumbent: Consulting an elder-law attorney before adding a name to an account.
- Evidence: "many families unintentionally create joint accounts when a convenience signer would be more appropriate" (https://seniorsafetyadvice.com/alternatives-to-joint-bank-accounts-for-elderly-parents/), 2025/2026.
- Severity: 4 — creates downstream benefits-eligibility and estate problems that surface later, when they are hard to unwind.

### 4. Monthly bill-pay and account monitoring is a recurring, unpaid chore
- Who: Adult child acting as informal money manager
- What hurts: Watching multiple accounts each month for missed payments, late fees, duplicate charges and zombie subscriptions with no dedicated tool; professional daily money managers estimate this workload even for straightforward cases.
- Frequency: Professional DMM clients need "approximately four hours of services per month" at minimum, more for complex finances.
- Cost (time/money): $25–$100/hour for a paid DMM, or flat fees such as $130/month or $50/month for bill-paying-only services.
- Current workaround/incumbent: Hiring a daily money manager, using bank bill-pay, spreadsheets, or free volunteer bill-paying through Area Agencies on Aging.
- Evidence: "costs for these services varies by region but it often ranges between $25 and $100 per hour" (https://www.elderlawanswers.com/daily-money-managers-can-help-assist-seniors-with-financial-matters-9167), and "Most clients need approximately four hours of services per month" (same source).
- Severity: 3 — steady time and money drain rather than a single crisis, but compounds monthly.

### 5. Comingling and record-keeping risk for informal proxies
- Who: POA agent, family caregiver
- What hurts: Guidance repeatedly warns proxies never to comingle funds and to "document everything," because failure to keep meticulous records can trigger accusations of financial elder abuse — but no tooling is described to make that record-keeping easy.
- Frequency: unknown; described as a standing rule, not an incident count
- Cost (time/money): Legal exposure if records are inadequate; unknown dollar amount.
- Current workaround/incumbent: Manual documentation, sharing records with siblings, spreadsheets.
- Evidence: "Document everything you do and keep meticulous records, sharing all information with your siblings, as such communication can prevent tension and legal challenges" (https://www.agingcare.com/articles/financial-power-of-attorney-manage-senior-expenses-155469.htm) [attributed via aggregated search summary; verify wording against source directly].
- Severity: 3 — low-frequency but high-consequence if it goes wrong (accusations, litigation).

### 6. Elder fraud losses are large and often discovered only after money is gone
- Who: Adult child / family watching parent's accounts
- What hurts: Fraud against people 60+ produced huge aggregate losses in 2024, and the mechanism (gift cards, romance/investment scams) is designed to move money instantly and anonymously so it cannot be recovered once discovered.
- Frequency: 147,127 elder-fraud complaints to FBI IC3 in 2024, a 46% increase in complaint count year over year.
- Cost (time/money): $4.885 billion in total 2024 losses; 7,500 victims 60+ lost over $100,000 each, averaging $83,000 in that high-loss group.
- Current workaround/incumbent: After-the-fact reporting to IC3/FTC; monitoring services like EverSafe or Carefull; family vigilance.
- Evidence: "Older Fraud Victims Lost $4.9 Billion in 2024" (https://www.aarp.org/money/scams-fraud/fbi-report-fraud-2024/), reporting on the FBI IC3 2024 data.
- Severity: 5 — largest, most irreversible financial harm in this territory.

### 7. Gift-card and romance scams drain funds over weeks before family notices
- Who: Adult child of a scam victim
- What hurts: A parent can be manipulated into repeated gift-card purchases over an extended period before the family becomes aware, by which point recovery is essentially impossible because gift-card funds are spent immediately.
- Frequency: unknown per-case duration; described generally as scams unfolding over "months" before discovery in reporting.
- Cost (time/money): One publicly reported case cited a $400,000 loss to a Facebook-originated scam before the family intervened [unverified — could not directly access primary article to confirm quote wording; figure appears in search-result summary of https://moneywise.com/life/lifestyle/mother-victim-to-facebook-gift-card-scam, 2024].
- Current workaround/incumbent: Reporting to FTC/retailer after the fact; account monitoring apps.
- Evidence: "by the time investigators try to track fraudsters, store surveillance footage is months gone and retailers have no records of who used the cards" (referenced in aggregated coverage of FTC gift-card fraud data; see https://consumer.ftc.gov/consumer-alerts/2023/08/if-you-paid-scammer-gift-card-your-money-gone-maybe-not).
- Severity: 5 — irreversible loss, and detection routinely lags the harm.

### 8. VA fiduciaries face accounting burden and audit risk
- Who: VA fiduciary managing a veteran's benefits
- What hurts: Fiduciaries managing more than $10,000/year or a 100%-rated veteran must file detailed annual accountings and report every change in the beneficiary's circumstances (address, income, hospitalization, incarceration, death); OIG has found the program "susceptible to abuse," implying scrutiny and paperwork risk for fiduciaries too.
- Frequency: Annual accounting requirement for qualifying cases; biannual 3-month Funds Under Management Review otherwise.
- Cost (time/money): 167 misuse cases found with over $618,000 reissued in one fiscal year, indicating the scale of oversight and rework involved. [figures describe programwide misuse, not per-fiduciary burden]
- Current workaround/incumbent: Manual recordkeeping, VA-provided guide (https://benefits.va.gov/FIDUCIARY/Fid_Guide.pdf).
- Evidence: "Fiduciaries must submit annual accountings if the funds under management or disbursed yearly for the beneficiary exceed $10,000 or the veteran is rated 100 percent" (https://www.congress.gov/crs-product/IF13019), 2024/2025 CRS product.
- Severity: 4 — recurring compliance burden with real penalty risk for the fiduciary.

### 9. Representative payees face SSA OIG financial audits of fund use
- Who: Social Security representative payee (often adult child)
- What hurts: SSA OIG conducts financial-related audits to check whether individual and organizational representative payees "used and accounted for Social Security benefits in accordance with SSA policies," meaning payees must be able to produce documentation on demand, sometimes years after the fact.
- Frequency: Recurring OIG audit program, both individual and high-volume payees, going back decades (audits dated from the 1990s through 2018 in the OIG archive).
- Cost (time/money): unknown per-audit cost or hours to families; the audits themselves are described as full financial reviews.
- Current workaround/incumbent: Manual bookkeeping, annual SSA accounting form.
- Evidence: "determine whether individual representative payees used and accounted for Social Security benefits in accordance with SSA policies and procedures and had effective safeguards over the receipt and disbursement of Social Security benefits" (https://oig-files.ssa.gov/audits/full/A-05-18-50587.pdf).
- Severity: 3 — infrequent per family but high-stakes and record-dependent when it happens.

### 10. Bank accounts freeze automatically on notification of death
- Who: Executor / surviving family
- What hurts: As soon as a bank learns of the death, the account is typically frozen "to protect the estate," blocking access to funds even for funeral costs, until documentation clears.
- Frequency: unknown industry-wide rate; described as standard bank practice.
- Cost (time/money): Duration until "the estate is settled" not specified by source; probate itself commonly runs months.
- Current workaround/incumbent: Requesting an early release for funeral expenses before probate completes; opening an estate account once a death certificate and executor proof are provided.
- Evidence: "Banks often freeze accounts once they're notified of the account holder's passing to protect the estate... preventing unauthorized transactions while the estate is in probate or until legal documentation is provided" (https://www.binehamgillen.com/blog/do-banks-freeze-accounts-when-someone-dies/).
- Severity: 4 — cuts off cash flow at the exact moment funeral and immediate expenses are due.

### 11. POA authority ends at death, leaving a gap before executor authority is recognized
- Who: Former POA agent, now often the executor
- What hurts: The POA automatically expires at death and cannot be used to close accounts, so the same person who managed the parent's money for years must restart the recognition process from zero with every institution as executor.
- Frequency: universal — applies at every death where a POA existed.
- Cost (time/money): unknown time cost quantified in sources, but implies duplicated paperwork across institutions.
- Current workaround/incumbent: Obtaining letters testamentary/probate documents and death certificates before re-approaching each institution.
- Evidence: "a power of attorney cannot close a bank account after the account holder's death, as the POA is no longer authorized to make decisions regarding the deceased person's finances once the account holder passes" (search-synthesized from https://www.helpwithmybank.gov/help-topics/bank-accounts/opening-closing-inactive-bank-accounts/closing-a-bank-account/closing-poa.html).
- Severity: 3 — procedural but forces the whole recognition fight to restart during grief.

### 12. Executors juggle many institutions each with its own death-notification process
- Who: Executor
- What hurts: Every bank, card issuer, brokerage and utility has its own process, certified-death-certificate requirement and turnaround time, and there is no single channel; digital accounts and unknown assets add further discovery work.
- Frequency: unknown exact count of institutions per estate in independent sources; Elayne (vendor) content lists five major banks as examples, implying a typical estate spans multiple large institutions plus smaller ones (utilities, subscriptions).
- Cost (time/money): unknown aggregate hours; vendor content states closing even "small tasks such as closing a bank account can seem like impossible feats" during grief. [vendor claim, not independently corroborated — flagged per brief's death-admin aggregator caveat]
- Current workaround/incumbent: Manual checklists (e.g., bank-provided "executor assist kits"), estate-settlement services such as Elayne, Empathy, Atticus.
- Evidence: "even small tasks such as closing a bank account can seem like impossible feats" (https://www.elayne.com/resources/how-to-close-a-bank-account-after-someone-dies), vendor content, undated (2026 site).
- Severity: 4 — high cumulative burden from repetition across many institutions, though single-source (vendor) evidence on the framing.

### 13. Debt collectors pursue former POA agents or family after death for debts they don't owe
- Who: Former POA agent
- What hurts: Collection agencies sometimes pursue the person who held POA for the deceased's debts even though POA authority (and personal liability) ended at death, requiring the agent to fight back in writing.
- Frequency: unknown
- Cost (time/money): unknown
- Current workaround/incumbent: Written dispute to the collector; CFPB complaint.
- Evidence: "I held a power of attorney for a man residing in a nursing home. After he died, the nursing home hounded me to pay the debt. Even though I wrote to them and told them that I was not liable, they referred the matter to a collection agency that did not even list the real debtor." (https://www.consumerfinance.gov/consumer-tools/educator-tools/resources-for-older-adults/financial-security-as-you-age/when-a-loved-one-dies-and-debt-collectors-come-calling/), CFPB.
- Severity: 3 — adds stress and correspondence burden during estate settlement, though not usually a real debt.

### 14. Monitoring-service sign-up friction undercuts the tools meant to help
- Who: Adult child trying to set up fraud monitoring for a parent
- What hurts: At least one reviewer of a leading elder fraud-monitoring service reported being unable to connect a bank account during signup and found cancellation required a phone call rather than the online process used to sign up.
- Frequency: unknown (single Trustpilot review found; only one review existed on that platform at time of search)
- Cost (time/money): Subscription itself runs $7.49–$24.99/month depending on tier.
- Current workaround/incumbent: Calling to cancel; trying a different monitoring vendor.
- Evidence: "signing up for a free trial online but was unable to connect their bank account; they noted that you can't cancel online and have to call" (https://www.trustpilot.com/review/eversafe.com), summarized from Trustpilot.
- Severity: 2 — onboarding/cancellation friction rather than core-function failure, but notable given thin independent review coverage of this whole product category.

### 15. Elder-law and DMM fees are a real out-of-pocket cost for oversight that many families can't verify is working
- Who: Adult child paying for professional help
- What hurts: Families turn to elder-law attorneys or daily money managers because self-management is error-prone, but professional help costs $25–$100/hour or a flat monthly fee, on top of the free options (spreadsheets, bank alerts) that require the family's own unpaid time instead.
- Frequency: ongoing, monthly.
- Cost (time/money): $25–$100/hour; flat fees such as $130/month or $50/month.
- Current workaround/incumbent: DMM services, spreadsheets, bank bill-pay/alerts, elder-law attorney retainer.
- Evidence: "Standard Bill-Paying services are $130/month" (https://www.elderlawanswers.com/daily-money-managers-can-help-assist-seniors-with-financial-matters-9167).
- Severity: 3 — recurring cost families weigh against DIY time cost, with no independent data on how well either option actually catches errors.

## Incumbents and why they fall short
- EverSafe (https://www.eversafe.com/, reviews at https://www.trustpilot.com/review/eversafe.com) — tiered $7.49–$24.99/month monitoring; thin independent review base (one Trustpilot review found) and at least one report of bank-connection failure and phone-only cancellation.
- Carefull (https://getcarefull.com/articles/how-to-get-a-bank-to-accept-your-power-of-attorney) — publishes its own advice on getting banks to accept a POA, which is itself evidence the underlying bank-acceptance problem is unresolved by the tool.
- Elayne (https://www.elayne.com/resources/how-to-close-a-bank-account-after-someone-dies) — death-administration content acknowledges the "impossible feats" framing of small closing tasks, but per the brief's own caveat this sub-niche rests heavily on this one aggregator's self-published comparisons (e.g., "Empathy vs Elayne," "Alix Review") with no independent corroboration found in this search pass.
- Daily money managers (AADMM-style services, e.g., https://www.elderlawanswers.com/daily-money-managers-can-help-assist-seniors-with-financial-matters-9167) — effective but costly ($25–$100/hr) and inconsistent in what "four hours a month" actually catches; no outcome data found.
- Bank-native tools (authorized signer, POA, joint account) — per https://seniorsafetyadvice.com/alternatives-to-joint-bank-accounts-for-elderly-parents/, banks routinely default families into the wrong relationship type, creating Medicaid and ownership problems later.
