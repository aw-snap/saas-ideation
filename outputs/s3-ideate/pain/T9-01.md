# T9-01 pain: confidential client material, lawyers

## Pain items

### 1. Cloud AI use risks privilege waiver
- Who: solo/small-firm lawyers drafting from client files
- What hurts: putting case facts into consumer ChatGPT/Claude can waive privilege because the tool is a third party with no duty of confidentiality.
- Frequency: unknown (no usage-frequency figure found)
- Cost: unknown; risk is loss of privilege on the underlying matter
- Current workaround/incumbent: avoid naming parties/facts, or avoid cloud AI entirely
- Evidence: "ChatGPT communications do not have legal privilege and cannot provide attorney-client confidentiality" (https://www.sfbar.org/blog/heads-up-new-chatgpt-privacy-concerns-for-lawyers-and-legal-staff/) — Sept 2025
- Severity: 5 — threatens the core professional duty (Rule 1.6), not just efficiency

### 2. Court ruling strips AI chats of privilege after the fact
- Who: any lawyer/client who drafted with an AI tool
- What hurts: a federal ruling held AI-drafted material isn't privileged or work product because the AI "owes no duty of confidentiality."
- Frequency: one reported case (US v. Heppner)
- Cost: unknown, case-specific exposure
- Current workaround/incumbent: none named; enterprise tools "might" fare differently
- Evidence: "an AI tool is not an attorney, owes no duty of confidentiality, and cannot form an attorney-client relationship" (WebSearch summary of https://www.joneswalker.com/en/insights/blogs/ai-law-blog/your-ai-conversations-are-not-privileged-what-a-new-sdny-ruling-means-for-every.html)
- Severity: 4 — a live legal-precedent risk, not hypothetical

### 3. Solo adoption low but rising, mostly via consumer tools
- Who: solo practitioners
- What hurts: adoption is climbing fast but concentrated in unmanaged consumer ChatGPT rather than vetted tools, per ABA survey trend.
- Frequency: 18% of solos used AI tools (2024) vs 10% (2023); 62% of solos "using or considering" ChatGPT
- Cost: unknown
- Current workaround/incumbent: ad hoc consumer ChatGPT use
- Evidence: "Solo practitioners demonstrated significant adoption increases, with 18% now using AI-based tools, compared to 10% in 2023" (https://www.americanbar.org/groups/law_practice/resources/law-practice-magazine/2025/july-august-2025/ai-adoption-trends-by-law-firm-size-solo-small-and-mid-sized/), 2025
- Severity: 4 — mismatch between eager adoption and unmanaged confidentiality risk

### 4. Unbilled review and drafting time is the majority of the day
- Who: solo/small-firm lawyers, all matter types
- What hurts: most of the working day goes to non-billed tasks, much of it document review and drafting, before any AI-confidentiality question even arises.
- Frequency: daily
- Cost: average lawyer bills 2.9 of 8 hours; utilization rate 38%
- Current workaround/incumbent: manual review, night/weekend catch-up
- Evidence: "the average lawyer records just 2.9 hours of an 8-hour day for billable work" (https://www.clio.com/resources/legal-trends/benchmarks/), 2025
- Severity: 4 — large, chronic time loss that any confidentiality-safe tool would need to address without leaking data

### 5. Contract document-review attorneys work at commodity rates
- Who: contract/staff attorneys doing discovery and production review
- What hurts: document review is billed and staffed as low-skill commodity work, at rates courts call barely above minimum wage.
- Frequency: per review project, often 40+ hrs/week
- Cost: $30–$125/hour typical; one court found "no reasonable paying client would accept a rate above $100 per hour for contract attorneys"
- Current workaround/incumbent: outside contract-attorney staffing agencies
- Evidence: "there is little excuse for delegating primary document review to anyone other than extremely low-cost, low-overhead temporary employees" (https://cloudnine.com/ediscoverydaily/case-law/is-a-blended-document-review-rate-of-466-per-hour-excessive-best-of-ediscovery-daily/)
- Severity: 4 — high-volume, low-margin work that firms already try to offload cheaply

### 6. Document-review contract attorneys fight for overtime
- Who: contract attorneys doing document review
- What hurts: attorneys hired purely for document review argue the work is routine enough that they should get overtime, meaning firms already treat it as non-professional, exhausting labor.
- Frequency: ongoing litigation pattern
- Cost: 40+ hrs/week, uncompensated overtime disputed
- Current workaround/incumbent: staffing agencies, overtime lawsuits
- Evidence: "contract attorneys hired for document review may be eligible for overtime pay when they work more than 40 hours per week and the work is basic enough" (https://www.newyorkcitydiscriminationlawyer.com/overtime-for-attorneys-hired-to-perform-document/)
- Severity: 3 — labor-cost pain adjacent to, not squarely inside, the confidentiality problem

### 7. Deposition summaries are billed by the page and add up fast
- Who: solo/small-firm litigators and their paralegals
- What hurts: outsourcing deposition summaries is the standard workaround for the time sink, but it costs per page and requires sending transcripts to a third-party vendor.
- Frequency: per deposition
- Cost: outsourced summary/transcript $3–$8/page, roughly $300–$1,500 per transcript; AI-vendor alternative advertised at $1/page
- Current workaround/incumbent: outsourced summary services (Ditto, SmartDepo), or the lawyer/paralegal doing it manually
- Evidence: "Traditional outsourced services typically charge $3–$8 per page" (https://magnals.com/how-much-do-depositions-cost/)
- Severity: 3 — real recurring cost, but a third-party vendor already absorbs the confidentiality question by contract, unlike ad hoc AI use

### 8. NYC Bar requires consent before AI records a client call
- Who: NY lawyers recording/transcribing client meetings with AI notetakers
- What hurts: informed consent must be obtained every time, and lawyers must "independently review" any AI transcript or summary for accuracy before relying on it — an extra step on every call.
- Frequency: every recorded client conversation
- Cost: unknown time cost; compliance burden not quantified
- Current workaround/incumbent: manual note-taking, or consent scripts before recording
- Evidence: "clients must be notified, and their consent obtained, whenever their calls are being recorded by an AI-empowered system" (https://www.nycbar.org/reports/formal-opinion-2025-6-ethical-issues-affecting-use-of-ai-to-record-transcribe-and-summarize-conversations-with-clients/), Dec 22, 2025
- Severity: 4 — a named, dated regulatory friction directly on the recording/transcription workflow

### 9. Lawyers must vet AI tools they supervise, including client-side use
- Who: solo/small-firm lawyers supervising staff or aware of client AI use
- What hurts: Rules 5.1/5.3 duties extend to making sure staff understand AI confidentiality risk, and lawyers must advise clients who record calls with their own AI tools of the risk of losing privilege — duties with no dedicated staff to carry out.
- Frequency: ongoing/every matter
- Cost: unknown
- Current workaround/incumbent: informal office policy, if any
- Evidence: "attorneys have a duty under Rules 5.1 and 5.3 to ensure that those they supervise understand their ethical duties and are competent to carry them out" (https://www.nycbar.org/reports/formal-opinion-2025-6-ethical-issues-affecting-use-of-ai-to-record-transcribe-and-summarize-conversations-with-clients/)
- Severity: 3 — compounds duties onto a lawyer with no compliance staff

### 10. Boilerplate consent language doesn't satisfy ABA Op. 512
- Who: any lawyer trying to get client consent to use GenAI
- What hurts: a lawyer cannot rely on a generic engagement-letter clause; informed consent must specifically describe what data goes in and how it could be used, which means a bespoke conversation per client/matter.
- Frequency: per client engagement
- Cost: unknown time cost
- Current workaround/incumbent: none reported; some firms skip AI use rather than draft bespoke disclosures
- Evidence: "merely adding general, boiler-plate provisions to engagement letters purporting to authorize the lawyer to use GAI is not sufficient" (https://thebarexaminer.ncbex.org/article/fall-2024/generative-artificial-intelligence-tools/), July 29, 2024
- Severity: 4 — turns a one-time policy fix into recurring per-client work for a solo with no support staff

### 11. Vendor due diligence is expected but hard for a solo to perform
- Who: solo/small-firm lawyers choosing an AI vendor
- What hurts: ABA guidance expects lawyers to read and assess vendor contract terms and security practices before using a tool on client data — work normally done by a firm's IT/security team, which solos don't have.
- Frequency: per vendor, ongoing as terms change
- Cost: unknown
- Current workaround/incumbent: trusting vendor marketing claims, or avoiding the tool
- Evidence: "It is very important to look at the vendor's contract (e.g., the OpenAI/Azure license terms), plus the vendor's ability to deliver on their contractual obligations" (https://zuva.ai/blog/aba-formal-opinion-512/)
- Severity: 4 — names the exact compliance work a solo cannot outsource

### 12. Enterprise-grade confidentiality safeguards are priced out of solo reach
- Who: solo and small-firm lawyers vs. Big Law
- What hurts: the AI tools with the strongest contractual confidentiality protections are sold only through organizational procurement, so solos are stuck with public-facing terms and no negotiating leverage.
- Frequency: structural, not episodic
- Cost: unknown dollar figure, described qualitatively
- Current workaround/incumbent: rely on "the settings available in the product" and personal discipline
- Evidence: "Large firms can ask procurement teams, information-security officers, and vendor counsel to negotiate data-processing terms, audit rights, and retention windows. Solo and small-firm lawyers often cannot." (http://jolt.law.harvard.edu/digest/ai-confidentiality-and-the-stratified-legal-profession)
- Severity: 5 — direct statement of the structural gap this territory targets

### 13. Flagship legal-AI tools are priced and bundled for large firms
- Who: solo practitioners evaluating CoCounsel
- What hurts: CoCounsel isn't sold standalone; it rides on a Westlaw subscription, pushing a solo's real monthly cost far above what the AI layer alone would cost.
- Frequency: monthly subscription
- Cost: roughly $428–$639/user/month once bundled with Westlaw; standalone AI layer quoted at $225–$400+
- Current workaround/incumbent: skip CoCounsel, use consumer ChatGPT instead
- Evidence: "The pricing structure clearly targets large firms and Am Law 100 operations. Solo practitioners and small firms get little benefit from the model." (https://thelegalprompts.com/blog/cocounsel-pricing) — vendor-adjacent analysis, treat as colour
- Severity: 3 — cost barrier pushes solos toward unvetted consumer tools

### 14. Add-on AI pricing on practice-management software isn't transparent
- Who: small-firm partners on Clio
- What hurts: Clio's AI assistant (Manage AI/Duo) is a quote-priced add-on on top of already per-seat pricing, so firms can't budget before a sales call, and per-user pricing scales painfully with headcount.
- Frequency: ongoing subscription decision
- Cost: a 5-attorney firm on Complete pays $695/month ($8,340/yr) for Manage alone; a 10-attorney firm on Advanced pays $1,490/month before AI add-ons
- Current workaround/incumbent: stay on base plan without AI features
- Evidence: "The most common complaint is pricing — particularly for firms that need multiple users on the Advanced or Complete plan" (https://checkthat.ai/brands/clio/reviews) — third-party review aggregator, treat as colour
- Severity: 3 — budget friction more than a confidentiality problem, but keeps solos on ungoverned tools

### 15. Enterprise legal-AI seat minimums exclude solos outright
- Who: solo/small-firm buyers of AI contract or research tools
- What hurts: several tools price at $1,000–$1,200/seat/month with 20–25 seat minimums, an annual commitment near $288,000 — categorically unreachable regardless of interest.
- Frequency: n/a, structural
- Cost: ~$288,000/year minimum commitment cited
- Current workaround/incumbent: cheaper unbundled tools or none at all
- Evidence: "some enterprise tools cost roughly $1,000 to $1,200/lawyer/month with a minimum of 20 to 25 seats, which totals an annual commitment starting around $288,000" (WebSearch summary; source article among https://elephas.app/resources/legal-ai-tools-pricing-comparison and similar) [unverified primary link]
- Severity: 4 — quantifies the price wall, though the underlying source is a marketing comparison site and should be treated as colour, not proof

### 16. Solo/legal-aid lawyers lack infrastructure for a privacy-preserving option
- Who: solo practitioners and legal-aid lawyers specifically named by researchers
- What hurts: even privacy-preserving academic tooling assumes local compute the majority of small practices don't have, leaving a hard choice between cloud risk and no AI at all.
- Frequency: unknown
- Cost: unknown
- Current workaround/incumbent: none identified; described as an open gap
- Evidence: "the majority of legal professionals—ranging from legal aid workers to solo practitioners—lack access to such advanced and costly infrastructure" (https://arxiv.org/html/2501.10915v1), academic paper, 2025
- Severity: 4 — independent, non-vendor source naming the exact hardware/skill gap

### 17. Malpractice carriers are now underwriting around AI use
- Who: solo/small-firm lawyers renewing professional-liability policies
- What hurts: carriers moved from silence on AI to coverage conditioned on governance, or outright exclusions for "AI-generated work product," adding a new compliance question at renewal.
- Frequency: annual renewal
- Cost: unknown premium impact; described as a market-wide shift
- Current workaround/incumbent: adopt a written internal AI policy to keep coverage
- Evidence: "Between January 2025 and January 2026, there was a 'structural break' in the professional liability market, with silence on AI in renewal documentation being replaced by affirmative-but-conditioned coverage, sweeping exclusions" (WebSearch summary of https://www.alpsinsurance.com/blog/insurance-coverage-issues-for-lawyers-in-the-era-of-generative-ai and related carrier guidance)
- Severity: 3 — adds insurance risk on top of ethics risk, though figures are unverified

### 18. Client trust in AI on legal matters is low, adding a client-facing cost
- Who: solo/small-firm lawyers whose clients learn AI was used
- What hurts: even where a lawyer complies with every ethics duty, a meaningful share of clients distrust AI involvement in their legal matter, so disclosure itself can cost client confidence.
- Frequency: per disclosed use
- Cost: unknown in dollars
- Current workaround/incumbent: non-disclosure (itself an ethics risk) or avoiding AI
- Evidence: "24% of Americans don't trust AI with any legal tasks... only 45% are comfortable sharing sensitive personal details with AI" (WebSearch summary of https://www.aol.com/articles/ai-trust-gap-42-ask-170019557.html) [unverified original survey source]
- Severity: 2 — real but softer, client-relationship pain rather than a workflow blocker

## Incumbents and why they fall short
- CoCounsel/Westlaw bundle: priced and structured for Am Law firms; a solo pays $428–$639/user/month once Westlaw is included (https://thelegalprompts.com/blog/cocounsel-pricing).
- Clio Duo/Manage AI: gated behind Essentials-or-above plans as a custom-quoted add-on, so small firms can't see a price without a sales call (https://checkthat.ai/brands/clio/reviews).
- Spellbook: no published pricing tiers, all quotes custom, which the source calls a source of budgeting unpredictability (https://www.dioptra.ai/resources/spellbook-prices-up-how-to-redline-past-agreements-affordably).
- Consumer ChatGPT/Claude: cheap and available, but carries the privilege-waiver and no-duty-of-confidentiality risk documented in items 1–2.
- Deposition/transcript outsourcing vendors (Ditto, SmartDepo): solve the labor problem but require sending the transcript to a third party, at $1–$8/page (https://magnals.com/how-much-do-depositions-cost/).
